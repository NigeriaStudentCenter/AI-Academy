What Exactly Is an AI Agent?

An AI agent is not simply a smarter chatbot. It is a system designed to pursue a defined objective, use relevant context and knowledge, interact with approved tools, take permitted actions, and operate within boundaries.

Goal

What outcome is the agent responsible for? A useful goal is specific enough to guide behaviour and narrow enough to manage safely.

Context

What does the agent know about the current customer, conversation, transaction and situation?

Knowledge

Which trusted information sources can the agent use, and how is that information kept current?

Tools

What systems can it query or interact with — for example CRM, order management, appointment systems or knowledge bases?

Actions

What can it actually do? Actions should be explicit, testable and permissioned.

Guardrails & humans

What must the agent refuse, verify, escalate or hand over to a person?

The Agent Anatomy

Think of an agent as a digital worker

What the agent needs

  • Purpose: a defined job to accomplish.
  • Context: information about the current situation.
  • Knowledge: trusted information it can rely on.
  • Tools: controlled access to systems.

What makes it safe

  • Actions: explicit permitted operations.
  • Guardrails: boundaries and refusal rules.
  • Oversight: escalation and accountability.
A useful design question is: “What does this agent need to know, what does it need to do, and what must it never do?”
2 · Short Teaching Video / HeyGen Script

The Anatomy of an AI Agent

Recommended length: 6–8 minutes · Presenter: professional educator.

Welcome back to the BSOE AI Academy. In Module 1, we explored the agentic revolution and the shift from AI that answers to AI that can take authorised action.

Now we need to answer a practical question: what actually makes an AI agent an agent?

Think about a human customer-service employee. They have a job to do. They understand the situation. They have access to information. They use business systems. They take certain actions. And they operate under policies, permissions and supervision.

A well-designed AI agent follows a similar pattern.

First: Goal. What is the agent responsible for achieving? “Help customers” is too broad. “Help customers track and resolve standard delivery enquiries” is much more useful.

Second: Context. The agent needs to understand the current conversation and relevant customer situation. Without context, an agent may give technically correct but practically useless answers.

Third: Knowledge. Knowledge is the information the agent can use — such as approved policies, product information, procedures or FAQs. Knowledge is not the same as a tool.

Fourth: Tools. Tools allow the agent to interact with systems. A CRM lookup, order-status system or appointment calendar can turn a conversation into a useful service.

Fifth: Actions. We must define what the agent is allowed to do. Looking up an order is different from cancelling an order. Drafting a refund is different from issuing a refund.

Sixth: Guardrails. Guardrails define the boundaries. They can include authentication requirements, financial limits, prohibited actions, confidence thresholds and escalation rules.

Seventh: Human oversight. Some situations should move to a person. This is not a failure of the agent. It is part of responsible design.

So remember the model: Goal, Context, Knowledge, Tools, Actions, Guardrails and Human Oversight.

In the next activities, you will use this model to take apart a real customer-service scenario and then design your first agent anatomy map.

The better you understand the anatomy, the better you will design the agent.

5 · Case Study

Case Study: The Insurance Claims Agent

The situation

An insurer wants to use an AI agent to improve the first stage of motor-claim handling. Customers report incidents through a digital channel. The organisation wants faster triage and fewer repetitive tasks for claims staff.

Potential agent role

Collect initial information, check whether required fields are complete, retrieve policy information, request permitted documents, summarise the case and route it to the appropriate claims workflow.

The boundary

The organisation does not want the agent making unrestricted final decisions on complex or high-value claims.

Why?

Claims may involve disputed facts, vulnerable customers, legal considerations, fraud indicators and significant financial consequences.

Agent Anatomy Challenge

ComponentPossible design
GoalCollect and triage standard claim information efficiently.
KnowledgeApproved claims procedures and policy information.
ToolsCRM, policy system, document-management system.
ActionOpen a claim record and request standard documents.
GuardrailDo not make final complex/high-value decisions.
EscalationRoute disputed, vulnerable, suspicious or high-impact cases to a trained employee.
Case lesson: The agent's value is not determined by how much authority it has. It is determined by how effectively its authority matches its purpose.
