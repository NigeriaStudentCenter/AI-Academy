AI Agents Need Governance, Not Just Guardrails

Deployment is not the end of the agent lifecycle. Once an agent interacts with customers, governance becomes an ongoing management responsibility. Organisations need to know who owns the agent, what it is allowed to do, how decisions are reviewed, how incidents are handled and how changes are approved.

Good governance does not ask only, “Can the agent do this?” It also asks, “Should it do this, who is accountable, what evidence do we keep, and what happens when it gets it wrong?”

Accountability

Assign named ownership for the agent, its data, actions, outcomes and incidents.

Risk

Identify foreseeable harms and apply proportionate controls before and during operation.

Privacy & security

Protect customer information and control access to data, tools and systems.

Transparency

Make the agent's role clear and provide appropriate routes to human support.

Auditability

Keep appropriate records so important interactions, changes and incidents can be investigated.

Continuous improvement

Use evidence from real-world operation to improve the agent without weakening controls.

The 8-Part Agent Governance Model

Governance areaKey questionExample control
Purpose & scopeWhy does the agent exist and where is it allowed to operate?Approved purpose statement and scope boundary.
OwnershipWho is accountable?Named business owner, technical owner and escalation owner.
Risk managementWhat could go wrong?Risk register with severity, likelihood and mitigations.
Data & privacyWhat information is processed and why?Data minimisation, access controls and approved retention approach.
Security & permissionsWhat can the agent access or change?Least privilege, authentication and controlled tools.
Human oversightWhen must a person intervene?Escalation rules and accountable human decision-maker.
Monitoring & auditHow do we know what is happening?Performance indicators, incident logs and appropriate records.
Change managementHow are prompts, knowledge, tools and policies changed?Versioning, testing, approval and rollback before release.
Governance principle: The more consequential the agent's actions, the stronger the evidence, controls and human accountability should be.
1 · Short Teaching Video / HeyGen Script

Who Is Responsible When the Agent Is Wrong?

Recommended length: 9–11 minutes.

Welcome to Module 8, the final stage of our AI Agents and Customer Engagement learning journey.

Your agent is now deployed. But deployment does not transfer responsibility to the machine. The organisation remains responsible for how the system is designed, used, monitored and changed.

Governance starts with ownership. Someone must be accountable for the business purpose. Someone must understand the technical operation. Someone must own escalation and incident response.

Next comes risk. Ask what could cause harm: inaccurate information, inappropriate disclosure, unfair treatment, unsafe recommendations, unauthorised actions, poor escalation or failures in connected systems.

Controls should match the risk. A simple FAQ agent may need relatively light controls. An agent that changes accounts, handles sensitive information or takes consequential actions requires much stronger controls and human oversight.

Governance also requires transparency. Customers should understand when they are interacting with an AI system where that information matters, and they should have an appropriate route to human assistance.

Then consider monitoring and auditability. You need enough evidence to understand what the agent is doing, identify patterns, investigate incidents and evaluate whether changes improve performance.

Finally, govern change. Agents evolve. Prompts change. Knowledge changes. Tools change. Policies change. Every meaningful change can alter behaviour, so important changes should go through an appropriate review, testing and release process.

The goal of governance is not to stop innovation. It is to make responsible innovation repeatable.

Should the Agent Be Allowed to Act?

Imagine a customer says: “I need to change my account details. Please do it for me.”

Agent capabilityGovernance decisionWhy?
Explain the processGenerally suitable.Low-risk informational support when based on approved knowledge.
Show the customer's existing detailsRequire appropriate authentication.Customer information should not be exposed to an unauthorised person.
Change the details automaticallyDepends on the process and risk.Identity, permissions, confirmation and auditability matter.
Override a security control because the customer insistsNo.Customer pressure should not bypass a control.

4 · Case Study

Retail Banking Support Agent

A fictional retail bank introduces an AI customer-service agent. It can answer product questions, explain transaction processes, retrieve authenticated account information and initiate selected service requests.

RiskGovernance responseEvidence / monitoring
Incorrect financial informationUse approved knowledge sources and escalation for uncertain/high-impact questions.Accuracy reviews, complaints and escalation patterns.
Unauthorised accessRequire appropriate identity verification and role-based permissions.Access logs and security alerts.
Customer disputes an agent actionProvide human escalation and preserve appropriate interaction context.Incident and complaint review.
Agent behaviour changes after an updateVersion changes, test before release and retain rollback capability.Release records and post-release monitoring.
Agent receives an unusual requestStay within scope and escalate rather than improvise.Out-of-scope and escalation reporting.

Governance board decision

The bank does not approve the agent simply because it passed functional tests. It reviews purpose, scope, risks, access, human oversight, monitoring, incident response and change management. The agent is approved for defined services, with stronger controls around consequential actions.

Case lesson: Governance should be designed into the operating model, not added as a document after the agent is already live.
